SUBSTACK-ONLY RESEARCH · COLLECTION UPDATE

The boundaries of acceptable dissent

Feasible as an observational study of expressed criticism and community responses. The data do not yet establish a general penalty for dissent.

Recommended question: When do political communities welcome criticism of their own side, and when do they treat it as undermining a shared cause? A sharper working title is Loyal Criticism and Coalition Discipline in Political Newsletter Communities.

Collection completed in this run

The article archive now has 9,035 records, with publicly returned prose on 3,891. This supersedes the initial 2,500-record pilot in coverage; the old collection is preserved. Each publication was extended to 1,000 posts or until its public archive endpoint returned no further new records. Paid bodies were excluded from the analytic corpus.

Comments were requested for 200 discussions across all ten publications. 171 returned comments, yielding 43,408 distinct publication/comment records and 20,799 replies. 29 requests returned empty results despite positive archive comment counts and are flagged as unresolved access/availability. They are not zero-dissent observations. The new comments are separate from the old 221,696-row Krugman export.

This comment batch is a purposive feasibility sample: up to 15 archive-ordered posts with at least 150 public words and positive comment counts, plus five additional discussions, excluding very recent posts. Some archive entries are old or pinned; this is not a calendar-balanced recent-post sample. Publicly accessible discussion, not access to every historical comment, defines coverage. Public text may be a digest, transcript, or media introduction rather than a full original essay. No recurring collection was scheduled.

Why the idea is testable

The comment responses include text, post IDs, comment IDs, timestamps, nested reply links, reaction counts, and some deletion/status fields. Article text gives local context. The normalized export has 0 missing referenced parents and 3,582 text-bearing root comments with direct replies. This provides many opportunities to observe how criticism is answered, though label frequencies and statistical power remain unmeasured.

The collector requested all_comments=true. That parameter is not independent proof of complete retrieval. Reported child counts were checked against returned children; a mismatch is a coverage warning rather than evidence of censorship. Even perfect tree consistency would not reveal comments absent from the public response.

Additional coverage warning: 30 returned comment nodes report more children than the response supplies. Affected discussions are flagged in threads.csv; deletion, hidden content, or response truncation cannot be distinguished from this alone.

What the inspected exchanges show

Ten selected cases were documented through a single-assistant qualitative review. These are existence checks and coding challenges, not a representative sample or an estimate of effects:

  • In the older Krugman collection, criticism of weak Democratic messaging receives an explicitly supportive reply.
  • A comment criticizing Biden/Harris and challenging Krugman’s election explanation receives sarcastic and substantive rebuttals. Rebuttal alone is not exclusion.
  • A new Heather Cox Richardson exchange contains competing criticisms of Democratic choices and a response discouraging a “circular firing squad.” This is an observable unity argument, but who a reply addresses can require reading sibling comments.
  • A Bulwark comment saying it disagrees with an article title actually reinforces the article’s attack on the same opponent. A simple disagreement detector would label it incorrectly.
  • Another Bulwark exchange names Biden and failure sarcastically. A literal party-name/negative-word rule misclassifies the target.
  • A Free Press comment challenges sourcing, but its relationship to a political in-group is unclear. It belongs in an explicit unknown category.
  • In a Borowitz political appeal, electoral pessimism receives both a hopeful counterargument and a reply linking it to giving up, with a personal insult. This suggests a separate hypothesis about policing pessimism that could undermine mobilization.
  • In a Reich discussion, broad criticism of both parties receives a supportive call for Democratic change as well as a clarifying question. Strong criticism is not uniformly rejected.

The evidence therefore supports measuring different forms of criticism and different forms of response, rather than presuming that dissent always gets punished.

The essential distinctions

Do not collapseCode separately
Criticism of an allied actor vs disagreement with the articleA writer may invite criticism of their own political side. Such comments can agree with the author.
Strategy vs policy vs legitimacy“Our party needs better messaging” differs from rejecting its policy or portraying it as equally illegitimate as its opponent.
Rebuttal vs social sanctionA factual correction is different from ridicule, accusations of betrayal, or calls to leave.
Commenter vs politician as targetAn insult aimed at a politician is not punishment of the person posting the comment.
Participation vs personal affiliationDo not infer that a commenter shares a party identity simply because they comment in a publication.
Silence/low likes vs rejectionThere are no impressions or negative-vote data; no reply or few likes does not demonstrate disapproval.

A defensible design using only scraped data

  1. Define the local political alignment from article evidence. Identify actors and positions explicitly supported or opposed. Keep mixed/unknown cases. Do not force every publication, especially multiwriter outlets, into one political camp.
  2. Blindly label root comments. Code target, relationship to the article, criticism type, reasoning, and tone without viewing reactions or replies. Then separately code replies for agreement, substantive disagreement, ridicule, and explicit unity or exclusion language.
  3. Compare comments within the same article. Use like-count rank and separate response outcomes. Account for comment timing, length, tone, and repeated participation. Include comments that receive no replies. Snapshot results remain associational.
  4. Test the substantive contrast. Is strategic criticism accepted more readily than criticism that challenges coalition legitimacy? Does an author’s own invitation to criticize change that relationship? Treat these as hypotheses, not pilot findings.
  5. Validate before scaling. Independently double-code an initial sample, assess reliability and class frequencies, and then size the main study around the number of clear criticism and norm-policing events. Test any automated classifier on held-out articles and publications. Exclude or separately analyze satire and multi-item digests.

What is ready, and what remains unresolved

Ready: article/comment joins, repeated-commenter keys, reply trees, timing and reaction fields, a provisional codebook, 60 randomly sampled eligible root-comment contexts, 22 separately enriched challenge contexts, and separate blank input/reply annotation sheets. The random sample is six eligible roots per publication, not a random sample of all Substack comments. The prepared sample is not a completed validation exercise.

Unresolved: reliable political-target alignment, the prevalence of actual internal criticism, how often explicit social sanction occurs, and the extent of access-related selection. No estimated dissent penalty, classifier accuracy, or power calculation is claimed. Deleted/absent content and historical moderation reasons cannot generally be recovered.

Assessment: proceed with an annotation and measurement pilot. The strongest immediate outcome is the kind of response criticism attracts. Claims about banning, private beliefs, lost subscriptions, or a causal punishment mechanism are unsupported. For an APSR-oriented contribution, the aim is a general account of how coalitions distinguish loyal criticism from disloyalty; the platform is the empirical setting.
