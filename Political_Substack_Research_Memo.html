2. Literature: what is established and where this project can contribute

This is a focused scoping review, not an exhaustive systematic review. Searches on 18 October 2026 combined Substack/newsletters with polarization, subscriptions, homophily, audience engagement, and political journalism; APSR searches covered ideological measurement, agenda setting, online news exposure, and persuasion. Primary journal pages, author/institutional repositories, the Tow Center, and official Substack documentation were preferred. Downloaded PDFs were checked for a valid PDF signature, readable pages, and extractable text. Five APSR papers and three related papers are saved.

Three conversations meet here: political information exposure and sorting; audience rewards for antagonistic rhetoric; and the economic organization of independent journalism. My inference from this review is that the useful contribution is to connect financial and participation rules to who gets heard and what writers produce. Simply assigning left/right labels to ten large newsletters would offer a much narrower contribution. A broader novelty search would still be needed before claiming this is the first study of any proposed mechanism.

1. Bond & Messing (2015), APSR 109(1), 62–78. Quantifying Social Media’s Political Space

Facebook endorsements place political actors and more than six million users on a common latent scale, with external validation. This supplies a measurement precedent, not permission to equate a Substack comment with endorsement. A publication map needs independent anchors and validation.

Review access: PDF downloaded

2. Barberá et al. (2019), APSR 113(4), 883–901. Who Leads? Who Follows?

Topic models and temporal analyses of legislators’ and citizens’ Twitter messages indicate responsiveness, especially to supporters. This motivates audience-feedback hypotheses. Lagged prediction in observational Substack data would still be vulnerable to shared news shocks.

Review access: PDF downloaded

3. Stier et al. (2022), APSR 116(2), 768–774. Post Post-Broadcast Democracy?

Browsing histories and surveys from more than 7,000 participants in six democracies associate intermediary use with political news exposure. A newsletter study must distinguish available content, distribution, and actual reading rather than presume that self-selection necessarily eliminates diverse exposure.

Review access: PDF downloaded

4. Velez & Liu (2025; online 2024), APSR 119(2), 1036–1053. Confronting Core Issues

Tailored experiments find attitude polarization under contentious, vitriolic counterarguments, but not lower-valence counterarguments. Distinguish disagreement, hostility, and attitude change. An angry comment count does not measure a change in a reader’s political attitudes.

Review access: PDF downloaded

5. Velez, Liu & Clifford (2026), APSR First View. Beyond Belief Change

Two experimental studies distinguish belief change from downstream attitude change and examine personally relevant beliefs. This supports a consent-based newsletter persuasion experiment with follow-up measurement, rather than interpreting engagement as persuasion.

Review access: PDF downloaded

6. Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden (2021), PNAS 118(26), e2024292118. Out-group animosity drives engagement

In 2.73 million Facebook and Twitter posts, outgroup references strongly predict sharing. The theoretical extension is whether direct financial support and gated discussion alter these rewards. Their engagement result does not establish that hostile newsletters generate subscriptions.

Review access: PDF downloaded

7. Grimmer & Stewart (2013), Political Analysis 21(3), 267–297. Text as Data

Automated content analysis requires task-specific validation and close reading. For this project, use blinded human annotation, held-out publications, and separate labels for policy position, outgroup hostility, disagreement, and evidence use.

Review access: Author PDF downloaded

8. Zilberstein (2022), Columbia Tow Center. Digital Platforms and Journalistic Careers: A Case Study of Substack Newsletters

The computational and qualitative case study identifies newsletters as career resources, alternative media models, or lifeboats. Substack’s influence is heterogeneous; author background and outlet organization belong in the design. A generic claim that newsletters change journalism is already well covered.

Review access: Full report HTML saved

9. Acosta Meneses & Gómez-Escalonilla (2025), Journalism and Media 6(3), 128. Substack, the New “Home” for Cultural Journalism

A qualitative study of cultural journalists in Spain examines community, professional alternatives, and literary mediation. It is useful for interviews and author–audience relationships, but its setting does not establish patterns in U.S. political newsletters.

Review access: Repository PDF downloaded

10. Hurcombe (online 2024; issue 2025), Digital Journalism 13(9), 1523–1534. Conceptualising the “Newsfluencer”

The conceptual framework links platforms, business models, labor, and participatory cultures. This helps distinguish personal brands from newsrooms. Only the publicly indexed abstract and bibliographic information were reviewed here; the full paper was not downloaded.

Review access: Abstract-level review

11. Guess et al. (2023), Science 381, 398–404. How do social media feed algorithms affect attitudes and behavior in an election campaign?

The reported experiment changes users’ feeds substantially without detecting changes in key polarization outcomes during the study period. This is a useful caution against assuming that changes in content or engagement necessarily imply attitude effects. The abstract was reviewed, not the complete paper.

Review access: Abstract-level review; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37498999/

3. Five papers worth developing

The rankings below are judgments about feasibility and contribution, not estimated publication probabilities.

1 · Best immediate project

Who Speaks for the Audience? Unequal Voice in Political Newsletter Communities

Research question: Does the apparent consensus in newsletter comments depend on a small group of repeat participants? The mechanism is unequal participation, not a presumed effect of the platform on citizens’ attitudes.

Design. Use the existing Krugman threads first, then replicate across publications. Compare comment-weighted stance and topic distributions with one-commenter-one-vote distributions within each post and month. Estimate concentration, effective number of participants, newcomer participation, reply reciprocity, and whether high-volume voices receive disproportionate likes. Compare top-level comments and replies separately.

Measurement and identification. Manually label a stratified sample for agreement with the article’s claims, disagreement, hostility, and evidence use. Keep author criticism separate from criticism of an opposing party. Use blinded double coding and held-out validation before scaling. Bootstrap by post or participant as appropriate, account for repeat observations, and report missing/deleted-text sensitivity bounds.

Feasibility and limit. Claim available: comment sections can overstate consensus among active participants. Claim unavailable: comments misrepresent all subscribers, unless a consent-based reader survey supplies that benchmark. Generalizing beyond Krugman requires more communities. This is feasible now as a pilot; the 37.12% finding is descriptive and not a finished paper.

2 · Strongest institutional mechanism

Paying to Speak: How Comment Access Rules Shape Political Discussion

Research question: Does a payment requirement change who participates and the balance between disagreement and hostility? Payment could discourage harassment while also excluding less committed or less affluent voices; either outcome is theoretically informative.

Design. Record post audience and write_comment_permissions separately. Compare paid-only and open discussion within writers and similar topics as an exploratory analysis. A credible causal design needs verified changes in access rules and suitable comparison communities, or a writer partnership that randomizes comparable discussion opportunities.

Measurement and identification. Primary outcomes: distinct participants, newcomers, participation concentration, issue disagreement, hostility, and reasoning quality at a fixed post age. For a verified policy change, assess anticipation, pretrends, concurrent moderation changes, topic selection, and compositional turnover. Do not infer historical gate changes from today’s settings on old posts.

Feasibility and limit. Existing Krugman variation makes this an excellent feasibility study, but gate assignment is not random. A prospective panel or experiment would turn the descriptive pattern into a stronger institutional argument. Readers’ actual wealth or payment status is not recoverable from their comments.

3 · Closest to subscriptions and content incentives

Does Outrage Pay? Financial Support Versus Attention in Political Newsletters

Research question: Are the rhetorical features that attract likes and comments also those that make readers willing to financially support a writer? The theoretical distinction is between an attention reward and a financial reward.

Design. Use the article corpus to characterize public rhetoric and construct realistic stimuli. In a preregistered consent-based experiment, vary policy explanation versus outgroup antagonism and keep topic, length, evidence, and author cues controlled. Measure willingness to subscribe, reading choice, perceived credibility, and attitude outcomes separately. A real purchase or voucher choice, if feasible and approved, is stronger than hypothetical willingness to pay.

Measurement and identification. Observational stage: author/topic/time controls and fixed-age engagement measures test associations, with sensitivity to public-text missingness and satire. Subscription conversion requires publisher analytics or a consent-based experiment; rounded public counts and a bestseller badge are insufficient.

Feasibility and limit. This extends engagement research to a financial mechanism, rather than simply reproducing “negative language gets attention” on another website. Do not claim observed subscriptions or revenue growth from this pilot.

4 · Best ecosystem mapping paper

Separate Audiences or Shared Publics? Political Communities Across Substack

Research question: Do publication communities overlap across issue positions and media traditions, or are there distinct insulated clusters? Separate author recommendations and hyperlinks from aggregate commenter co-participation.

Design. Expand to a documented sample across U.S. Politics, News, World Politics, and other relevant categories, including smaller and inactive outlets. Select 50–100 publications using explicit size and ideological strata and preserve inclusion probabilities where possible. Build publication-to-publication overlap networks from pseudonymized participation; code the publication content independently.

Measurement and identification. Measure overlap relative to degree-preserving null networks, community structure, bridging publications, and robustness to dropping prolific users. Validate publication-level ideological dimensions using human-coded issue positions and explicit public self-descriptions; allow economic, cultural, institutional, and foreign-policy positions to differ.

Feasibility and limit. Commenting does not equal endorsement, subscription, following, or reading. Do not label ordinary commenters’ individual political identities from their participation. The current ten are an elite category sample and cannot establish the ideology of the entire platform.

5 · Ambitious longitudinal project

Audience Capture or Editorial Leadership? Feedback in Political Newsletter Production

Research question: Do writers’ subsequent topics and rhetoric track their comment sections, particularly their most active voices? Compare responsiveness to high-volume and occasional participants.

Design. Build writer-by-topic time series from original articles and comments; measure comments in fixed windows before subsequent articles appear. Model later topic attention and tone while controlling for prior writer attention, shared news events, writer schedules, and publication differences. Separate the response to an article from comments that introduce a new topic.

Measurement and identification. Lagged prediction is evidence of temporal association, not proof of influence. Common news shocks, unobserved readership, and writer selection remain serious alternatives. A writer partnership that randomly varies the feedback summary a writer sees could directly test responsiveness, subject to an appropriate research protocol.

Feasibility and limit. This connects agenda-setting work to subscriber-funded opinion leadership. It needs a longer balanced panel and stronger identification than the initial archive. It should follow the measurement and participation papers rather than be the first claim made from this collection.

Completed bounded pilot: 2,500 unique publication/post records (250 each), including 1,125 records with publicly returned text, 1,267 paid-audience metadata records, and 108 public-audience records with no text returned. Paid bodies were not collected into the analytic corpus. Body availability does not certify a complete prose article; media posts may contain little or no prose. A fixed number of recent posts covers very different time spans because publication rates differ. posts_common_window.csv restricts the pilot to 23 September–18 October 2026 New York time, producing 910 records within the observed coverage of all ten. This is a useful starting window, not proof that the platform supplied every published item. Counts are snapshots taken during collection; likes and comments cannot be fairly compared without controlling post age or prospectively revisiting at fixed ages.

Archive/API pages, RSS, robots.txt responses, request timestamps, HTTP outcomes, hashes, and status files are stored in the project folder. The collector waits 1.2 seconds before requests, checks publication robots.txt, logs failures, caches archive pages, and stops a publication on an error. It can resume against the frozen ranking; changing the pilot limit expands the same cohort. No recurring job was scheduled.

6. Recommended next sequence

  1. Make the Krugman pilot analysis ready. Join the three exports, preserve raw values, treat “None” as missing, reconcile comment-count definitions and collection timing, verify old/migrated records, and document why 62 posts lack comments. Do not overwrite the original files.
  2. Preregister a focused participation study. Define concentration and stance-weighting estimands; label a stratified pilot of around 500 comments plus article context. Revise and double-code before selecting a larger annotation sample. Preserve sarcasm and thread context. Do not call a convenience sample of comments “the audience.”
  3. Expand breadth and standardize time. Add publications across political categories and size strata, then collect a shared calendar period. Separate original newsletters, podcasts/video, livestream notices, and satire. Byline-level attribution matters for multiwriter outlets.
  4. Collect discussion and access rules prospectively. Save retrieval times, pagination evidence, post-age snapshots, and explicit missingness reasons. A proposed 24-hour / 72-hour / 7-day panel requires a later collection run; it is not already implemented or scheduled.
  5. Develop the payment mechanism with a partner or experiment. Seek actual conversion/access-policy evidence before making claims about subscription incentives. Establish consent and data-handling procedures for reader surveys and experiments. Publish aggregate or pseudonymized results rather than identifiable commenter dossiers or searchable excerpts.

Decision: Start with “Who Speaks for the Audience?” as the immediately executable paper. Develop “Paying to Speak” as the main theoretical project if a valid policy change or writer partnership can be secured. Treat ideology mapping as infrastructure and a possible ecosystem paper once the sample is broader.
