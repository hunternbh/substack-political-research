Competing audience expectations and editorial authority

Expanded manual pilot · October 19 2026 · Saved Substack data only · No model API calls or new API spending

The first angle remains promising as a study of how readers and writers define editorial obligations. The expanded sample reveals conflicts over evidence, source transparency, political responsibility, emotional support and independence from an echo chamber. It does not yet establish differential author accommodation or audience-caused position change.

Rewritten ASR draft · Word draft (local file) · Full coding decisions (local file)

What was added

I manually reviewed 118 new top-level comments across 24 selected publications. Two roots per publication were sampled randomly before three additional lexical screens selected candidates, without looking at response counts. Seventy screened candidates and 48 random roots were retained. One random root turned out to be a staff service message; it remains in the selection audit and is excluded from the 117-reader denominator. Earlier pilot roots were excluded. The v2 and v3 main samples now contain 286 distinct selected roots, including that staff message.

Selection covers public saved articles with 150–6000 words and root comments with 5–800 words, dated October 18 2024 through October 11 2026. Publications are purposively chosen and screen strata overlap before sequential deduplication. This is not a representative top-fifty estimate. Each parent text was read completely, with article openings and relevant source passages checked. Parent labels and a context addendum were frozen before response extraction. All 12 selected response texts were then read. This is one assistant’s exploratory coding, with no independent human agreement estimate.

Cases that develop the argument

Independence can be an audience demand. A Free Press reader objects that a personalized For You feed undermines the publication’s promise to expose readers to uncomfortable ideas. Three sampled peers agree. This is pressure against pleasing readers, which complicates a single audience-capture interpretation.

Traceability can conflict with emotional protection. Robert Hubbell declines to link an alarming election article because he regards it as unhelpful and does not want to reward its publisher. A reader requests the article’s name and newspaper so they can decide for themselves. A peer defends avoiding negativity and maintaining morale. The conflict concerns who controls readers’ attention and assesses credibility; no author reply to the request is captured.

Political solidarity can conflict with consistent standards. A Joyce Vance reader urges fighting fire with fire rather than maintaining moral standards toward an accused Democratic candidate. A Sykes reader contests criticism of Hunter Biden’s pardon by invoking protection against MAGA. Neither announces cancellation, and neither receives a captured matched-author reply. These are demands and disagreements, not observed author concessions.

Emotional rules are contestable. A Pantsuit Politics listener rejects a perceived demand to suspend criticism after Charlie Kirk’s assassination while explicitly condemning violence. The saved episode transcript supports analysis of the hosts’ emphasis on humanity, grief and online restraint; it also contains more nuance than a blanket ban on discussing harmful views. A sampled peer validates the listener’s complex emotions. This belongs inside the editorial-obligations argument, rather than being counted as proof of partisan extremism.

How this changes the ASR paper

The central question is: under what conditions do audience members gain the authority to define what political writers owe them? The proposed mechanism is that writers recognize some demands as legitimate contributions to their work, while defending an alternative conception of that work against others. Financial support, expertise, coalition loyalty and care can supply competing grounds for a claim. Peer replies participate in this process; they should not be collapsed into writer responses.

The earlier 53 paired exchanges document possible response practices, including correction, clarification, defense and service provision. They were selected because an author account replied and therefore cannot show which challenges receive replies more often. The new sample corrects that design problem, but sparse author responses mean the key comparative hypothesis remains untested. The strongest current use of the material is a theoretically focused qualitative pilot plus a specified larger study.

The rewritten draft separates completed exploratory observations from proposed tests. Earlier 4,268-comment dissent results are preserved in an archived draft, not relabeled as evidence for the new argument. Claims about actual subscription transactions, unseen moderation, causal stance change or ideological differences across the whole political spectrum remain unsupported.

Cost estimate if separately authorized later

Official OpenAI GPT-6 Luna documentation, checked October 19 2026, lists $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens at Standard short-context rates. Batch and Flex are half those rates. This estimate assumes two contextual passes per episode, each with 6,000 input and 1,000 total billable output tokens, plus a 25% reserve. Output includes any reasoning tokens. No caching discount is assumed.

If later authorized, 50000 screened roots plus 10000 two-pass contextual episodes would be about $36.25 Standard or $18.13 Batch including a 25% reserve on both stages. These volumes are planning choices, not a power guarantee.

At the assumed contextual workload and 25% reserve, $10 covers about 3600 Standard episodes or 7200 Batch episodes; screening would reduce those quantities.

These are model-token estimates, not a quote or an approved budget. They exclude researcher validation time, tax and account credit purchase requirements. A full-corpus run is a different workload. For two independent groups, approximately 293 observations per group detect a response-rate difference of .20 versus .30 at two-sided .05 and 80% power under a normal approximation. Allowing a design effect of two gives about 600 per domain before missingness and misclassification. This is illustrative; clustered and sparse author responses require simulation based on a larger validated pilot. Do not design power from the earlier sample conditioned on writer replies.

No paid call is authorized or has been made for this expansion. The earlier stop flag and API audit ledger remain in place. The public official documentation page was consulted for prices; free publisher abstracts and metadata were also checked for the rewritten draft.

For comparison, screening every one of the archive’s 1,256,544 comment records at 800 input and 120 output tokens each would be about $220 Standard or $110 Batch with the same reserve, before detailed contextual coding. That includes nested and out-of-window records, so it is a workload scenario rather than the recommended design.
