What this expansion changes

Five more clear loyalty demands, from four accounts in two discussions. Three tell a writer to protect a coalition or its principles; two tell other readers to restrain criticism. Four occur under Brian Tyler Cohen’s criticism of Democratic restraint; one occurs under Stone’s discussion of her break with Tucker Carlson. None has a captured response within seven days, even when we include deeper branches.

That still leaves too few independent cases for a dependable comparison. Across this and the preceding own-side pilot, we now have fourteen writer-directed demands, from fourteen accounts in seven publications and nine discussions. Including peer-directed restraint gives nineteen comments, seventeen accounts, eight publications and eleven discussions. These are a descriptive case ledger across different sampling designs—not pooled rates. The expansion adds Cohen as a publication with a strict writer-loyalty case; it does not add another Trump-supporting publication with one.

The substantive question is sharper now: when do readers treat political criticism as a service, and when do they treat it as a breach of obligation? Twenty-four new comments explicitly defend a writer’s independence. Loyalty itself can mean protecting a leader, protecting a coalition, or holding leaders to its principles. A demand for MAGA fidelity can therefore encourage criticism of Trump rather than forbid it.

What indirect replies add—and what they do not

105 focal comments are themselves nested replies. Two of the five strict loyalty claims would be missed by sampling starting comments only. Separately, I followed every saved descendant under each focal comment for seven days. Twenty-four focal comments have deeper conversation; two gain a captured-response classification only when we look beyond direct replies. There are 268 with no direct reply and 266 with no response anywhere below them.

Thirty-one focal comments have a matched writer somewhere below them: twenty-five at Kelly and six at Public Notice. Thirty have a direct writer reply. The one indirect-only writer case answers an intervening reader, so it is not evidence that the writer answered the focal criticism. No strict loyalty demand receives a matched writer reply.

I read all matched writer response texts and selected early peer responses in relevant branches: 47 focal–response context reviews, covering 44 unique texts. Public Notice acknowledges some criticisms, points to earlier coverage, and defends the article’s limited scope; it also corrects a reader’s mistaken claim that Gaza was omitted. Kelly promises continued advocacy and says she will emphasize an existing point on X after praise. The latter is an observable intention, not a verified later post or a change in stance.

These are saved nonself, nondeleted text responses within seven days. Deeper conversation can address somebody else. One affordability-cancellation comment has a reported missing-child flag and no captured response, so absence there is especially uncertain. Captures can be incomplete even without such flags. Unknown staff or cohost accounts can evade byline matching.

Three cases worth building around

1. Reader pressure and editorial choice can pull in several directions

In A Grand Ratio, Stone describes unsubscribes and angry notes when she shared Tucker Carlson videos. She says she first removed them from the main page and later decided to stop sharing them after developing her own reservations. That supplies a first-person account linking pressure, compromise and an editorial decision. Yet she also gives substantive reasons for changing her mind. Without earlier versions, exposure measures or a counterfactual, the sequence cannot isolate audience-caused adaptation.

In its new comments, many readers affirm listening despite disagreement. One wants stronger MAGA principles and accountability; another wants paid-only commenting to exclude alleged hostile infiltrators. The latter is a demand about community access and moderation, kept separate from a demand to change the writer’s position. A reported intention to upgrade financially is not a verified transaction.

Read Stone’s article

2. Criticism intended to strengthen a coalition can still be called disloyal

Cohen argues that Democratic procedural restraint helps Republicans win. Some readers instead ask him to criticize Republicans and cheer Democrats; some police other readers who criticize the party. Others challenge the article’s legal reasoning without making a loyalty demand. This is a clean contrast between rival definitions of helpful criticism within one discussion, although the strict cases receive no captured responses.

Read Cohen’s article

3. Content of criticism and identity of its target both matter

Loury questions overreach in DEI rollbacks, campus-speech enforcement, tariffs and military intervention. The saved comments contain disagreement, but no new strict loyalty case. Some complaints address his anti-Trump cohost John McWhorter or other guests, not Loury. Kelly’s pieces mostly encourage tougher action on already shared goals; readers often applaud and she replies frequently. Those episodes help identify aligned reinforcement, but should not be treated as equivalent to a writer breaking with Trump.

The dog discussion adds another mechanism: people may relate to the writer through care and companionship rather than political discipline. These differences make a simple left-versus-right response rate difficult to interpret.

Do we now have enough loyalty cases?

No—not for the proposed response comparison. The new five claims are only four accounts and two disputes, with no captured responses. Across the two own-side expansions, fourteen writer-directed claims are still concentrated, and response opportunities differ sharply by writer. A large total comment count cannot substitute for enough independent loyalty episodes.

The strongest current paper direction is contested obligations in reader-supported political commentary: readers negotiate whether they are buying analysis, coalition service or access to an independent voice. The pilot documents these competing expectations and one promising author-described editorial sequence. It does not yet show which demand wins, that either political camp exerts more pressure, or that pressure changes later writing.

For a stronger study within the retained sixty, the next step is to build an inventory of explicit current-ally criticism in all accessible posts, then match each episode to saved discussions and later same-topic writing. Keep a random baseline at every depth, code who is being criticized and which coalition is defended, and distinguish requests, threats, praise and actual author responses. Where there is no saved discussion or later comparable writing, record that limit rather than infer absence of pressure. Independent human recoding and enough separate writers/disputes are essential before comparative claims.
